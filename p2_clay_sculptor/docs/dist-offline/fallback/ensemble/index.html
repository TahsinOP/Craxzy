Asking the Simulator

Guiding question

A learned model is cheap and sometimes wrong. The simulator is right and expensive. Can the model itself say when it is about to be wrong, well enough that the simulator is needed only then?

Five models instead of one

Section titled “Five models instead of one”

The structured model of part V is trained five times. The data, the architecture and the optimiser are identical; only the random seed differs, which changes the initial weights and the order of the mini-batches. This is a deep ensemble (Lakshminarayanan et al., 2017).

The five members were all pushed towards the same training targets, so where the data constrain the answer they agree. Where the data are thin, or the situation is unlike anything in the training set, nothing forces them to agree, and each lands wherever its own initialisation took it. Their spread is therefore a measure of how much the answer is the data’s and how much it is an accident of training.

For a candidate action aaa the members predict height maps H^1(a),…,H^5(a)\hat H_1(a), \dots, \hat H_5(a)H^1​(a),…,H^5​(a). Two numbers are taken from them.

The prediction is the mean map, and its cost is what the search minimises:

Hˉ(a)=15∑m=15H^m(a),c(a)=RMSE⁡(Hˉ(a), H⋆).(1)\bar H(a) = \frac{1}{5}\sum_{m=1}^{5} \hat H_m(a),\qquad c(a) = \operatorname{RMSE}\big(\bar H(a),\, H^\star\big). \tag{1}Hˉ(a)=51​m=1∑5​H^m​(a),c(a)=RMSE(Hˉ(a),H⋆).(1)

The disagreement is the spread of the members, pixel by pixel, summarised as one length:

u(a)=1322∑i,jVar⁡m[H^m(a)]ij .(2)u(a) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{32^2}\sum_{i,j} \operatorname{Var}_m\big[\hat H_m(a)\big]_{ij}}\,. \tag{2}u(a)=3221​i,j∑​Varm​[H^m​(a)]ij​​.(2)

Both are in millimetres. ccc says how good the action is believed to be; uuu says how much that belief should be trusted.

The fallback rule

Section titled “The fallback rule”

The search runs exactly as in The Planner, with (1) as its cost. It ends with 96 evaluated candidates, sorted by predicted cost. Then:

  1. Compute the disagreement u(a∗)u(a^\ast)u(a∗) of the best candidate.
  2. If u(a∗)≤τu(a^\ast) \le \tauu(a∗)≤τ, execute a∗a^\asta∗. No simulation is used.
  3. If u(a∗)>τu(a^\ast) > \tauu(a∗)>τ, simulate the eight best candidates with MuJoCo, and execute the one whose true result is closest to the target. The stopping rule of the planner is then applied to that true cost, so the simulator also decides whether to stop.

A firing costs 8 simulated primitives. A decision of the pure oracle costs 96. The threshold τ\tauτ sets how often the rule fires: τ=∞\tau = \inftyτ=∞ never asks (the pure learned planner), τ=0\tau = 0τ=0 always asks.

The re-ranking can only choose among candidates the model already liked. If the model’s eight favourites are all poor, the simulator picks the least poor. The oracle is used as a judge, not as a search.

A second signal: doubt about the decision

Section titled “A second signal: doubt about the decision”

The disagreement uuu measures doubt about a shape. The planner does not execute a shape, it executes a choice: the candidate ranked first. A re-ranking can only change the outcome if the true order of the top candidates differs from the predicted one, and an error that all candidates share does not change the order. If every member predicts every dent 0.3 mm too shallow, uuu may be large while the ranking, and therefore the decision, is right.

So a second signal looks at the ranking directly. With gjg_jgj​ and sjs_jsj​ its mean and standard deviation over the members, and a normal fit to the five samples, the probability that aja_jaj​ is in truth better than a1a_1a1​ is Φ(−gj/sj)\Phi(-g_j / s_j)Φ(−gj​/sj​), where Φ\PhiΦ is the standard normal distribution function. Both signals are computed at every decision of every run and recorded, so that afterwards each can be tested against what a re-ranking actually gained.

The control experiment

Section titled “The control experiment”

A fallback rule can help for two different reasons: because the simulator is consulted at the right moments, or simply because it is consulted at all. To separate them, every triggered planner is compared with a random one that fires with a fixed probability, at three rates. If a signal carries information, the planner it triggers should reach a lower error than the random planner at the same number of simulator calls.

The thresholds are set from the signal itself: the 25 %, 50 % and 75 % quantiles of uuu and of pmisp_{\text{mis}}pmis​ over all decisions of the pure learned planner, so that each triggered planner fires at roughly 75 %, 50 % and 25 % of decisions, the same rates as the three random planners.
